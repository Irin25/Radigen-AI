[Pleural Effusion | Definition]
Pleural effusion is an abnormal build-up of fluid in the thin space between the lung and the chest wall, called the pleural space. A small amount of fluid is normal there, but larger amounts can compress the lung and make breathing harder.

[Pleural Effusion | X-ray appearance]
On a standard upright chest X-ray, fluid tends to pool at the bottom of the chest, so the earliest sign is blunting of the costophrenic angle, the normally sharp corner where the diaphragm meets the chest wall. Larger effusions produce a curved white line rising up the side of the chest (a meniscus) and can obscure the diaphragm outline. On a film taken lying down, fluid instead spreads out as a general haziness over the lung rather than forming a clear line.

[Pleural Effusion | Common causes]
Common underlying causes include heart failure, infection or pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, and cancer. Kidney or liver disease can also lead to fluid accumulation throughout the body, including the pleural space.

[Pleural Effusion | Often seen with]
Frequently observed alongside atelectasis (partial lung collapse) and underlying lung consolidation, since collapsed or infected lung tissue and fluid often occur together.

[Pleural Effusion | Look-alikes]
Can be confused with pleural thickening, a raised hemidiaphragm, or severe lower lobe consolidation, all of which can also blunt the costophrenic angle without true fluid.

[Pleural Effusion | Limits of the X-ray]
Small volumes of fluid, especially less than a few hundred milliliters, can be missed on a standard upright frontal film. An X-ray also cannot tell whether the fluid is simple, infected, or bloody; that requires further imaging or sampling of the fluid.
